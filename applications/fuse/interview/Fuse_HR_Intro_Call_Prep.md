## Your positioning

> **I turn ambiguous, high-value operational problems into practical, auditable systems that teams can actually use.**

Do not position yourself only as a Data Scientist who builds models. Position yourself as a technical operator who can frame a problem, analyse evidence, build a fit-for-purpose solution, align people, and drive it into use.

## 1. Fuse Energy and the role

### The company in 30 seconds

Fuse Energy is a London-based full-stack energy company founded in 2022 by Alan Chang (CEO) and Charles Orr (COO), both formerly at Revolut. Its stated goal is to make energy abundant and affordable by building across renewable generation, batteries, grid infrastructure, real-time power trading, customer supply, distributed-energy hardware, and consumer products.

The key idea is vertical integration: Fuse aims to control more of the chain from generation to customer supply, reduce friction and cost, and use technology to respond to real grid conditions.

### What this Founder’s Associate role is really asking for

This is not a conventional executive-assistant role. It is a founder-office / operator role: take an unclear, important business problem and move it from **question → structured analysis → decision → implementation**.

- Analyse trading, supply economics, and operational bottlenecks.
- Scope new markets, products, regulatory environments, or development opportunities.
- Build a model, internal tool, decision memo, or operating process—depending on what the problem actually needs.
- Prepare founder-level decision material.
- Own cross-functional special projects without needing a detailed playbook.

### Your fit narrative

> At Li Auto, I have repeatedly taken difficult engineering and operational questions through problem framing, data design, analysis or ML, reusable infrastructure, deployment, and internal productisation. I am most effective where technical work must become a real decision workflow for engineering, operations, and business teams.

### Culture note

Fuse clearly values speed, high standards, directness, and ownership. Treat this as a high-intensity founder-led opportunity. Do not raise negative public reports in an HR intro; use the questions below to understand the actual working model.

### Q5. Tell me about a project you are proud of.

**Primary story: thermal-runaway risk ranking**

> One project that best represents how I work was a thermal-runaway risk project at Li Auto.
>
> The challenge was that thermal-runaway events are extremely rare. We had only a small number of known failure cases against a fleet of hundreds of thousands of normal vehicles. A conventional binary classifier would have created a misleading impression of precision, and it would not have given engineers the explanation they needed for investigation.
>
> I reframed the problem as explainable relative-risk ranking. The objective was not to claim that we could predict an exact probability of failure. It was to identify vehicles whose battery behaviour and usage patterns looked most similar to known failure patterns, so that teams could prioritise investigation and diagnosis.
>
> I designed the feature-processing and platform-normalisation logic, built an interpretable statistical scoring framework, and structured it as a monthly batch workflow with data-quality checks, training, scoring, explanation, publication, and audit records.
>
> Importantly, each risk score could be decomposed into feature-level contributions, so analysts could see why a vehicle ranked highly rather than just receiving an opaque score. On our validation, 93% of newly observed failure cases appeared within the top 2% of risk-ranked vehicles.
>
> I then helped turn that output into an internal workbench used by battery, quality, and after-sales teams for cohort comparison, individual-vehicle review, and root-cause investigation.
>
> The main lesson for me was that the best technical solution is the one that fits the operational decision. In this case, interpretability, reproducibility, and usability mattered more than maximising a conventional model metric.

**If asked: What did you personally own?**

> I owned the project end to end: problem framing, data and label design, modelling decisions, feature engineering, pipeline design, validation, explanation design, and the translation of the output into an internal workflow. I worked closely with battery-domain and after-sales colleagues, but I was accountable for the technical and product logic.

### Q6. Tell me about a project involving business impact or stakeholders.

**Battery longevity / causal decision system**

> Another project was a battery-longevity decision system.
>
> The business wanted to understand which real-world charging and usage behaviours had a meaningful impact on battery degradation, so that the findings could inform BMS or OTA strategies, battery design boundaries, and after-sales planning.
>
> The difficult part was avoiding a simple correlation story. Driver behaviour, temperature, vehicle usage, and battery conditions are highly confounded, so I used propensity-score matching, balance diagnostics, and treatment-effect estimation to distinguish more plausible causal effects from confounders or intermediate variables.
>
> I converted the work into reusable monitoring and decision workflows, then worked with BMS, cell-design, performance, and after-sales teams to translate the insights into feasible strategies.
>
> It taught me how to make a technical analysis useful for a real decision, while being disciplined about what the evidence can and cannot claim.

### Q7. Tell me about a disagreement or how you persuaded stakeholders.

**NLP work-order classification story**

> A good example was an after-sales work-order classification project.
>
> We had more than 15,000 noisy free-text work orders, inconsistent terminology, and a class structure that had to be meaningful to after-sales experts.
>
> We initially explored more complex language-model approaches, but the results were not strong enough and, more importantly, they did not fit the experts’ need for transparency and control over the taxonomy.
>
> Rather than forcing a more sophisticated model, I worked with the after-sales team to refine a 30-plus-category taxonomy and built a hybrid approach using expert rules, TF-IDF features, and a Random Forest model.
>
> We also created a feedback loop: low-confidence predictions were sent for human review, verified labels flowed back into training, and error cases were jointly analysed.
>
> The result was an improvement in operational accuracy from 85% to 96% within three months, while replacing roughly twelve hours of weekly manual triage.
>
> The lesson was that the best solution is not always the most complex one. It has to be explainable, operationally sustainable, and trusted by the people who use it.

> I have not worked directly in the UK power market, so I would not claim that I could immediately make domain decisions on pricing or trading without learning the context.
>
> What I do bring is a strong pattern of getting up to speed quickly in complex technical domains. In battery intelligence, I had to understand physical systems, operational constraints, fleet data, safety risks, and the needs of multiple stakeholders before I could build anything useful.
>
> My approach would be to learn the unit economics, operational metrics, and decision cadence quickly; speak to the people closest to the workflow; identify where the bottleneck really is; and then bring structured analysis and execution to it.
>

### Q9. How do you work with ambiguity?

> I start by separating the problem into three things: the decision we need to make, the evidence we have, and the constraints that matter.
>
> I then try to define the smallest useful version of the solution rather than over-designing from the beginning. I usually work in short loops: clarify the decision, test the key assumption, get feedback from the people closest to the problem, and then build only what the next decision requires.
>
> In ambiguous situations, I think clear communication is especially important. I make explicit what we know, what we do not know, what assumptions we are making, and what would change the recommendation.

### Q10. What would you do in your first 90 days?

> In the first 30 days, I would focus on understanding the value chain, the operating cadence, the key metrics, and the highest-cost bottlenecks. I would spend a lot of time with the teams closest to operations rather than assuming I understood the problem from a dashboard.
>
> Between days 30 and 60, I would aim to deliver one concrete piece of value — perhaps a decision analysis, an internal tool, or a process improvement — that solves a real pain point and earns trust.
>
> By 90 days, I would want to own a high-priority cross-functional problem end to end, with a clear metric, a working rhythm with stakeholders, and a practical plan for scaling the solution.
